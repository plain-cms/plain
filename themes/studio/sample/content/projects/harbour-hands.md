The trust looks after the working end of the harbour: the slipway, the patent crane, the last boatyard still launching wooden hulls. For its fortieth year it wanted an exhibition about the people rather than the machinery — and it wanted it outdoors, where those people would walk past it every morning.

## The challenge

Shipwrights, riggers and ferry skippers are not sitters. Nobody had a free hour, nobody wanted a studio light in the yard, and the trust was clear that it did not want heritage pictures: no sepia, no rope, no gazing out to sea. The prints also had to survive a winter on a north-facing quay.

## The approach

Tomas spent six weeks in the yards with one camera and one lens, and photographed each person for exactly as long as their tea break lasted. Every portrait is framed the same way — hands and face, the tool they reached for first, daylight from the shed door. We printed all forty at life size on enamelled steel, the same material as the harbour's own depth markers, and hung them along the quay wall in the order the day shift arrives.

## The outcome

The exhibition was due to come down after three months; the harbour master asked for it to stay, and it is now permanent. The trust's membership rose by a third over the summer, and eleven of the forty sitters have since led yard tours — which had never once been oversubscribed before.
