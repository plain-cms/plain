The first draft of the Colden valley map had north at the top, because that is where north goes. It was correct and nobody could use it.

The valley runs from south-west to north-east, and everyone who walks it starts at the bottom, by the mill, and climbs. On my draft the walk went diagonally across the sheet, the path names had to be set at an angle, and the two people I tested it on both turned the paper round in their hands before they had read a word.

## Turning the sheet

So I turned it for them. The mill is now at the foot of the page and the moor at the head; you walk up the paper as you walk up the hill. North points to the upper left, and a plain arrow says so.

> A map is a set of decisions about who is holding it, and where they are standing.

Medieval maps put east at the top, towards Jerusalem. Harbour charts are often drawn as the pilot sees the coast, from the sea. North at the top is a convention worth keeping when the reader will lay your map beside another one. A walker with one sheet in a wet hand is not doing that.

## What it cost

Two things. The grid lines now cross the sheet at an angle, which looks untidy until you stop noticing it. And one retired surveyor wrote to say I had drawn the valley wrong. I had not. But I added a second, smaller north arrow in the opposite corner, and he has not written again.

— Ines
