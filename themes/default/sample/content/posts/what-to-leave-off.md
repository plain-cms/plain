A survey gives you everything: every wall, every culvert, every change of surface. The map-maker’s work begins when you start throwing it away.

I keep a rule from my first year at the agency, given to me by a draughtsman called Ted Arkwright who had been inking sheets since before I was born. He would hold a draft at arm’s length and say only, “What is that for?” If you could not answer, it came off.

## Four questions

For each thing on a draft I now ask, in this order:

1. Will the reader pass it?
2. Will they need it to decide something — a turn, a crossing, a place to stop?
3. Could they mistake it for something else that is on the map?
4. If it were missing, would anyone notice?

Three walls in a row fail the first question twice. A single gate in a mile of fence passes all four.

## The hard ones

The difficult cases are the true things that mislead. A footbridge that exists but is closed. A path that is a right of way on paper and a bog in practice. I leave the bog path on, drawn thinner, with a note. Leaving it off would be tidier, and it would be a lie the first walker to find the stile would catch me in.

The best compliment a map of mine has had was from a woman who used it for a week and said she could not remember what it looked like. She had been looking at the hills.

— Ines
