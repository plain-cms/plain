The brief was one line long: a visitor map of the estate, showing every tree. The head gardener had counted them. There were eleven hundred and six.

I said yes, because it was January and the diary was empty, and spent a fortnight proving to myself that it could not be done. At any size a visitor could unfold, eleven hundred tree symbols are a green fog. The paths vanish. The lake becomes a rumour.

## Asking what it was for

I should have asked on the first day what the trees were *for*. When I finally did, the answer was not the one I expected. The estate did not want visitors to find trees. It wanted its own staff to stop losing them: which lime had been lopped, which oak was due an inspection.

That is not a visitor map. It is a register with a plan attached.

## Two maps

So we made two:

- **The visitor map** shows forty trees — the ones with names, the ones you would walk across the park to see — and every path, gate and bench.
- **The tree plan** is a plain black sheet at four times the scale, each tree a numbered dot, tied to the gardener’s ledger. Nobody will ever frame it. It is used every week.

The second map took a quarter of the time and is the one they thank me for.

I now ask the same question before I quote for anything: who will be holding this, and what will they do next?

— Ines
