Last autumn I stood by a car park for a morning and watched twelve people open one of my maps. I had a notebook and a flask, and one thing I wanted to know: who reads the legend?

Nobody did. Not one of the twelve so much as unfolded the panel it was printed on.

## Symbols that explain themselves

If the key is not read, every symbol has to carry its own meaning. I went through the sheet and asked of each one whether a stranger would guess it.

| Symbol | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Footpath | green dashes | black dashes, the word *path* every half mile |
| Stile | a small S | a drawn stile, two posts and a step |
| Boggy ground | blue stipple | blue tufts, the old agency symbol people know |
| Viewpoint | a red star | an open fan pointing at the view |
| Café | a cup | the name of the café |

The last row is the one that taught me most. A cup symbol says there is tea somewhere near. The name says which door to push.

## What the legend is for now

I still print a key, but it has become a different thing: half the size, and it lists only what could not be made obvious — the scale, the date of survey, and what a dashed boundary means. Everything else has to manage without it.

— Ines
