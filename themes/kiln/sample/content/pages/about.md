Ruth Calloway trained at a production pottery in Stoke-on-Trent, throwing four hundred mugs a week to somebody else's pattern. In 2014 she took the lease on the old dye house at Low Mill, built a gas kiln in the yard and began making her own.

## The clay

The body is a grey stoneware from a pit in Staffordshire, wedged with a little red clay so that iron spots bloom through the glaze in the firing. No two pieces speckle alike.

## The glaze

There is one glaze: a celadon of feldspar, whiting, china clay, flint and one per cent of iron oxide. Fired with the kiln starved of air, the iron turns the glaze from straw yellow to grey-green. It pools darker in a thrown ring and breaks pale over a rim.

Every new batch is tested first on a clay tile, dipped at one end and hung on a nail by the kiln door. The board of tiles by the door goes back to the first firing.

## The firing

| Stage | What happens | How long |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Biscuit | Dry pots are fired to 1,000 °C so they can be handled and glazed | 10 hours |
| Glaze | Dipped pots are fired to 1,280 °C, the last four hours in reduction | 13 hours |
| Cooling | The kiln stays shut until it is under 150 °C | 2 days |

## Seconds

A pot with a pinhole, a kiln scar or a warp is sold as a second at about half price. Nothing with a crack leaves the studio.
