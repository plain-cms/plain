There is a mailbox ninety seconds from my front door, bolted to the corner where the bus turns. For years every letter I sent went into it within a minute of being sealed, usually while I was on my way to something else, and I never once thought about it afterwards. That was the problem, though it took me a while to see it as one.

In June the box was taken away for a month — the corner was being repaved — and a notice sent us to the post office on Superior Street, twenty minutes’ walk downhill. I was annoyed for exactly one trip. On the second I noticed that I was carrying the envelope in my hand rather than my bag, and that somewhere around the bakery I had started going back over what was in it.

Twenty minutes is long enough to reread a letter in your head. Not the words — you can’t recover the words — but the letter’s manners: whether it asked anything of the person, whether it answered what they had actually said or only what was convenient, whether the joke in the second paragraph was a joke or a small unkindness wearing a hat. Twice that month I turned around. Once to add a line. Once, and I am gladder of this, to take one out.

The box came back in July, bright and newly level. I still use the post office.

I have tried to work out what the walk supplies that the corner did not, and the best I can do is that a letter has two halves and I had been skipping the second. Writing is the half where you say it. Sending is the half where you agree to have said it. The mailbox at the corner made the second half so short that it disappeared; the envelope was gone before I had finished meaning it. Twenty minutes puts the agreeing back. By the time I reach the counter I have either signed the thing twice or I am on my way home with it.

It has made me wonder what else I send from the corner. The message answered at the red light. The opinion delivered before the other person has finished the sentence. All of it posted within ninety seconds of being sealed, and none of it carried anywhere.

The clerk on Superior Street knows me now. She weighs the envelope, though it never needs weighing, and says “Going far?” and I tell her where. Then I walk back up the hill with my hands empty, which is the other thing the corner never gave me: the particular lightness of having sent something on purpose.
