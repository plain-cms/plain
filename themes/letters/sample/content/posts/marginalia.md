The field guide cost four dollars and came with a previous owner. I didn’t notice him until I got it home: a soft pencil, a slanting hand, a note beside nearly every bird. *Seen at the quarry, May 1971. Ours are smaller. Not this one — the wing bars are wrong.* On the page for the cedar waxwing he had written only “At last,” and underlined it twice.

I was raised to keep books clean. My mother covered our schoolbooks in brown paper and could find a dog-eared corner from across the room; writing in one was a thing other families did, like leaving the television on during supper. So for forty years my books have gone back on the shelf looking exactly as they did in the shop, and I had always counted that as respect.

The pencil man changed my mind in an evening. His guide is not a better guide than a clean copy; half his notes are wrong, and he corrects himself in the margins three years later with evident irritation. But it is the only book I own that has someone in it. I know which birds he waited for. I know he argued with the illustrator. I know that in the spring of 1974 he stopped going to the quarry, because the notes move to a lakeshore and the handwriting gets larger, and I can guess why handwriting gets larger.

A clean book records that it was owned. A marked one records that it was read — on what day, in what mood, by whom. I had been confusing the two, and treating my own reading as a kind of trespass that ought to leave no trace.

So I have bought a soft pencil. The rules are few. No underlining without a word beside it, because an underline alone only says *this*, and in ten years I will not know this what. Dates, always. Disagreements in full. And nothing I would be ashamed for a stranger to find, which turns out to be a useful rule for more than margins.

It is slower. A chapter takes half again as long when you have to decide what you think of it before you turn the page. But the books have started to answer back: I opened one last week that I had marked in June and found a note that said “No — ask Ruth.” I have no idea what I meant to ask her. I called her anyway.

Someone will pay four dollars for these, eventually. I would like them to get a previous owner thrown in.
