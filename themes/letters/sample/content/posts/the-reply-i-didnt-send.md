On the ninth of July I wrote the best letter of my life and did not send it. It was to a man who had been careless with something of mine, and it was fair, and it was funny, and every sentence in it landed exactly where I had aimed. I read it through twice with real pleasure. Then I put it in the drawer.

The drawer is a rule I took from my grandmother, who kept one in the hall table and called it the cooling rack. Anything written in heat went in overnight. In the morning it could be posted, if you still wanted to; she said that in fifty years she had posted about one in ten, and regretted two of those.

What the drawer does is not what I expected. I had assumed it worked by letting the anger drain off, so that the morning reader would be a calmer and kinder person than the evening writer. Sometimes that happens. More often I wake up exactly as angry, open the drawer, and find that the letter has changed instead. The fairness is still there. The jokes are still good. But overnight it has become obvious who the letter was for, and it was not the man.

A letter written in heat is addressed to an audience: the imaginary jury that will read it over the recipient’s shoulder and find for the plaintiff. Every line that pleased me most was a line for the jury. Take those out, and what remained was two sentences — *that was mine, and you were careless with it; I would like you to know that it mattered* — which I had not written, because they are not clever and they leave you standing there with your hands open.

I sent the two sentences. He telephoned within the hour, which the good letter would never have made him do. A man can admire a brilliant letter and file it. Two plain sentences have to be answered.

The good letter is still in the drawer. I can’t quite throw it away; it really is very funny. But I have started to think of the drawer less as a cooling rack than as a kind of sieve, and of the heat as something more useful than my grandmother allowed. The heat gets the letter written. The night finds out who it was written to. And the morning, if you are lucky, hands you back the two sentences you actually owe someone, small enough to carry to the post.
