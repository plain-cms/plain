Most people who walk the Pennine Way do it in one push of sixteen to nineteen days, and most of them walk it north. I am doing half of that.

North, because the hard country comes late — the Cheviots at the end, when the legs are made — and because the weather on these hills mostly arrives from the south-west, so it hits you in the back rather than the face. Every guidebook says so, and every guidebook is right.

## Why in pieces

In pieces, because of my left knee. It was operated on four years ago and it is good for about two long days in a row, then it wants a week on the sofa. So: two days, home on the train, three or four weeks off, back to the same stile.

The first five trips look like this:

| Trip | Days | From and to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| April | 1 and 2 | Edale to Standedge |
| May | 3 and 4 | Standedge to Ponden |
| June | 5 and 6 | Ponden to Malham |
| July | 7 and 8 | Malham to Hawes |
| August | 9 and 10 | Hawes to Middleton-in-Teesdale |

After that the stations thin out and the plan gets vaguer. The aim is Kirk Yetholm before the clocks go back.

> The way is long, but you only ever walk the bit in front of you.

That was my father's, said about most things. It seems to apply.

— Ruth
