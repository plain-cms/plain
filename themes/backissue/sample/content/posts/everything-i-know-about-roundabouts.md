My driving instructor, a patient man called Graham, told me on the first lesson that Stevenage was the best place in England to learn, because once you could do the roundabouts here you could do anything. Then he made me go round the same one eleven times.

The town was planned for cars and for people, kept apart. Cars got the ring roads and the roundabouts; people got the cycle tracks, the underpasses and a town centre with no traffic at all, the first in the country. It mostly worked. It still mostly works, if you are a car.

## A short field guide

- **The plain one.** Four exits, good sightlines, a bed of shrubs in the middle that the council trims twice a year. You will forget it the moment you leave it.
- **The double.** Two roundabouts joined by a short stretch, so that you leave one and are already in the next. Graham called it the figure of eight and said it separated drivers from passengers.
- **The magic one.** Not ours, strictly; there is a famous one in Swindon. But every new town has a junction where five roads meet and nobody, including the people who drew it, is sure who has priority.
- **The one with the sculpture.** See [The Sculpture Nobody Asked For](/blog/the-sculpture-nobody-asked-for/), in this issue. Usually concrete, usually from 1966, usually loved.

## What they are for

The planners wanted traffic to flow, and it does. What they did not plan for was how a roundabout sets the pace of a town: everything comes in, slows, looks right, and goes on. I passed my test on the fourth try. I still go round the big one by the station twice, some evenings, for no reason at all.
