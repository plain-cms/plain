The Water Gardens were made to be seen from above. The architect laid them out in steps below the old town hall, so that the councillors at their windows would look down on pools and fountains and the town they had made. The town hall has gone, and there is a shopping centre where it stood, but the gardens are still there, a little lower than everything else, like a room someone forgot to clear.

I went on a Thursday in September because the forecast said rain all day. I wanted to see them the way they mostly are.

## Two to five

The fountains were off. The rain filled the pools anyway, and made the surface of each one a different grey. A woman in a council tabard swept leaves off the steps with a broom that was nearly too wide for them.

At three, a group of schoolchildren came through in a line holding a rope, in yellow coats, and their teacher stopped them by the long pool and asked them what they could see. A boy at the back said, “Water.” She said, “Good,” and they went on.

At four, the rain stopped for eleven minutes. Two pigeons came down to the edge of the top pool, and a man on the bench across from me took out a sandwich and put his umbrella down, and the town went on above us, through the glass doors of the shopping centre, without looking down.

## What they are for

I had always thought of them as a view, something for people in offices. Sitting in them for three hours I decided they are more like a pause: the only place in the town centre where nothing is being sold and nobody needs you to move along.

At five the fountains came on, for no one, and I went home.
