The plan gave every neighbourhood the same things in the same order: a primary school, a parade of shops, a health centre, a church and a pub. They built ours in 1956 and called it the Kestrel, after the birds that hunted over the fields before the houses came. The sign had one on it, wings out, painted by someone who had clearly seen a kestrel.

It closed on the last Saturday in March. The brewery sold the building to a company that converts pubs into flats, and the company put a notice in the window that used the word “opportunity” four times.

## The last night

About a hundred people came, which is more than came on most Saturdays for the last ten years, and that is part of the story. Linda behind the bar had been pulling pints there since 1989. She let people take the beer mats. Someone took the dartboard. The darts team, which had won the league in 1997 and talked about it most weeks since, played one last game against nobody.

At eleven Linda rang the bell, and nobody moved, and she rang it again.

## What a pub was for

It was not really about the beer. The Kestrel was where you heard who was ill, who had a job going, whose son was back from the army. It was where the tenants’ association met, and the pigeon club, and, for one strange year, a group that wanted to bring back the trams.

The parade still has the chemist and the chip shop. The school is still there. The pub sign has been taken down, and the kestrels, the real ones, still hunt over the playing fields in the evening.
