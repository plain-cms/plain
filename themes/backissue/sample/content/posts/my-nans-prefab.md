The prefabs were built in factories after the war and bolted together on site in a day: two bedrooms, a bathroom with a real bath, a fitted kitchen with a fridge, which most of their tenants had never owned. They were meant to tide people over for ten years until proper houses were ready. My nan moved into hers in 1949 and left it in 1989, feet first, as she had always said she would.

It stood at the end of a row of eight, on the edge of what became the town, and the town grew up round it like a tide coming in.

## Inside

What I remember is the kitchen. A pale green cabinet with sliding doors that stuck in damp weather. A table that folded down from the wall. The fridge, which she called the refrigerator to the end, and which held milk, butter, and a single bottle of advocaat for Christmas.

The walls were thin enough that you could hear the rain on them, all of them, not only the roof. In winter she lived in the kitchen with the oven door open. In summer she lived in the garden, which was long and narrow and full of runner beans.

## Proper houses

The council offered her a proper house three times. She turned it down three times. A proper house, she said, had stairs, and she had seen what stairs did to people.

The row was taken down the year after she died. There are four semis there now, with garages. I walked past last month and someone in the end one, where her garden was, has planted runner beans.
