Nobody on the road knew it was coming. One morning there was a lorry and a small crane on the green, and by teatime there was a figure there, two metres tall and grey, a woman with her arms folded, looking at the houses.

My grandmother, who was not easily impressed, went out in her slippers to look at it and came back and said it was a disgrace. Then she said it looked like Mrs Dunn from number 40, which it did. Within a week it was called Mrs Dunn, and it has been Mrs Dunn ever since, though the real Mrs Dunn died in 1988 and the sculpture has a proper name on a plate that nobody reads.

## Art for everyone, whether or not

The idea, I learned later, was that good art should not be locked in galleries in London. A new town would have it on its greens and in its precincts, where anyone could walk past it on the way to the shops. It was a generous idea and a slightly bossy one, like most ideas of that time.

People complained. There were letters to the paper. Someone painted a moustache on Mrs Dunn in 1971, and someone else, I would like to think a neighbour, scrubbed it off before the council came.

## Why she stays

Children have climbed her for sixty years. Couples have had their photographs taken with her. When I left for university she was the last thing I saw out of the back of the car, arms folded, unimpressed, and when I came back she was the first.

Last year somebody from the council came to talk about moving her to the town centre, where more people would see her. The road had a meeting. She is staying where she is.
